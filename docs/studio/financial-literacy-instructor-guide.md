# Money in Real Life — Instructor Guide
## Financial Literacy Simulation Week · Our City / Rose City Studio

**Audience:** the coach/instructor running the room.
**Schedule:** 4 hours/day, Monday–Thursday, plus the Friday field trip.
**Materials for every day:** printed case packets (one per student: `/studio/curriculum/financial-literacy-case-packet.md`), event cards cut apart, role cards, chart paper + markers, sticky notes, timer, devices for the Studio checkpoint (last hour only — keep them closed until then).

**Standing rules for the room:**
- Practice numbers, never personal disclosures. Nobody has to reveal their family's finances — the cases carry the numbers.
- "Helping at home" is always framed as a legitimate, respectable budget line. Never a hardship narrative.
- Feedback language is student-safe: "hard to picture," "needs a number," "unsupported." Never "wrong," never "irresponsible."
- Teams of 3–4. Deal role cards Day 1 and keep the same roles all week — the week is one continuous financial life.
- You choose which event cards hit which teams. Match difficulty to where a team is, not to who's loudest.

**Scoring:** teams earn points daily on the scoreboard (see `financial-literacy-scoreboard-rubric.md`). Post it. Update it publicly at end of day.

# DAY 1 — Your First Check Is Not Your Whole Check

**Big idea:** the gap between gross pay and take-home pay is the first financial surprise of working life — and the first check comes with pressure already attached.

**Learning objectives.** Students can: (1) compute gross summer income from wage × hours × weeks; (2) estimate take-home at ~75% and explain where the rest goes; (3) build and defend a first-check plan that survives one surprise expense.

**Materials:** packets, event cards 1–8, role cards, chart paper, calculator-free first round.

### Time breakdown (240 min)

| Min | Block |
|---|---|
| 0–15 | Hook: the check reveal |
| 15–35 | Mini-lesson: gross vs. take-home |
| 35–50 | Teams form, roles dealt, Case rounds 1–2 (guess, then math) |
| 50–85 | Round 3: First-Check Plan |
| 85–95 | **Break** |
| 95–105 | Surprise card hits |
| 105–130 | Revised plan |
| 130–145 | Debrief circle |
| 145–175 | **Platform checkpoint: Take-Home Pay module** |
| 175–195 | Coach clinic |
| 195–225 | Share-out + scoreboard |
| 225–240 | Exit ticket + close |

### Facilitator script & moves

**Hook (0–15).** Board prompt as they walk in: **"$15.50/hr × 21 hrs × 6 weeks = how much is YOURS?"**
Script: *"Everybody write a number down. No math, gut only. That number in your head right now — that's the number stores, apps, and your friends are counting on. Let's find out how wrong we all are."* Collect a few guesses out loud, write them on the board, no judgment.

**Mini-lesson (15–35).** Teach only: gross pay, take-home pay, where the difference goes (federal/state taxes, FICA — name them, don't drown in them), and the 75% rule of thumb the Studio calculator uses. Board math, one pass: $15.50 × 21 × 6 = **$1,953 gross** → ×0.75 ≈ **$1,465 take-home** ≈ **$244/week**.
Say it plainly: *"Nobody stole from you. This is the price of the systems everyone uses — roads, schools, your grandmother's Social Security. But if you plan around $1,953 and live on $1,465, the gap comes out of YOUR plans."*

**Case rounds (35–85).** Teams work the packet. Round 3 is where the real content is: transportation, food, phone, helping at home, savings goal. Circulate.
**What to listen for:** teams that budget $0 food ("I just won't eat" — push: *"Show me the Tuesday you work 1–6pm"*); savings written last and smallest; whether "helping at home" sparks real talk (protect the tone — it's an option with dignity, not an obligation).

**Surprise card (95–105).** One card per team from cards 1–8. Script: *"This is not a punishment. This is a Tuesday."*

**Revise (105–130).** Teams rewrite the plan. Require one written sentence: what we cut and why.

**Debrief (130–145).** Circle. Questions in order:
1. What did you think was yours that actually wasn't?
2. What did your team protect? Why that?
3. Who cut savings first? Be honest. What would make you cut it last?

**Platform checkpoint (145–175).** Now devices open. Students go to `/studio/lab/financial-literacy/take-home-pay`, enter the case numbers (or their real wage if they have one and want to), confirm the calculator agrees with their hand math, and **save their reflection** — the two reflection fields map straight to the debrief.
Say: *"The module is your receipt. The case was the work; this is where you save it."*

**Coach clinic (175–195).** Small-group or whole-room, driven by what you heard: common picks — reading a pay stub (bring a sample), what FICA is in one sentence, why the first check is often smaller (partial period), direct deposit vs. check-cashing fees.

**Share-out (195–225).** Each team: 60 seconds — original plan, what the card broke, final plan. Score on the board (realism, protection of essentials, revision quality).

**Exit ticket (225–240).** Index card: *"One sentence: what did you think was yours that actually wasn't? One number: your realistic weekly take-home this summer."*

### Common misconceptions
- "Taxes are theft / I'll get it all back." — Some comes back at refund time if over-withheld; FICA doesn't.
- "Minimum wage jobs aren't worth budgeting." — $1,465 badly planned is $0 by August; well planned it's a car down payment.
- "Savings is what's left over." — Flip it: savings is a bill you pay yourself first.

### If time runs short
Cut the second share-out round, never the surprise-card revision — the revision IS the lesson.

### If tech fails
The whole day runs on paper. Have students photograph their First Check Plan; they complete the Take-Home Pay module tomorrow at the start of Day 2's checkpoint block.

### Collect
Exit tickets, one First-Check Plan per team (photo is fine). Skim tonight: tomorrow's $1,300 Month lands harder if you reference their actual cuts.

# DAY 2 — The Budget Is the Decision

**Big idea:** a budget is a set of tradeoffs under pressure, made before the pressure arrives.

**Learning objectives.** Students can: (1) allocate a fixed $1,300 across competing real categories; (2) revise under two successive shocks without abandoning essentials; (3) defend tradeoffs out loud with reasons, not vibes.

**Materials:** packets, event cards 1–10 (two rounds today), chart paper per team, sticky notes for gallery walk.

### Time breakdown (240 min)

| Min | Block |
|---|---|
| 0–15 | Hook: "$1,300. Go." |
| 15–35 | Mini-lesson: needs/wants/savings + the decision frame |
| 35–75 | Budget round 1 |
| 75–85 | **Break** |
| 85–95 | Event card #1 hits |
| 95–115 | Rebudget #1 |
| 115–125 | Event card #2 hits |
| 125–145 | Rebudget #2 + team defense prep |
| 145–170 | Gallery walk + sticky feedback |
| 170–200 | **Platform checkpoint: Budget Builder** |
| 200–220 | Coach clinic |
| 220–240 | Defenses + scoreboard + exit ticket |

### Facilitator script & moves

**Hook (0–15).** Board: **"$1,300 hits your account on the 1st. Rent isn't your problem yet — everything else is. What's your first move?"** Take answers cold. Someone will say "sneakers." Thank them for the honesty — you'll use it Day 3.

**Mini-lesson (15–35).** Needs vs. wants vs. savings vs. giving/helping — and the truth that categories are easy until two of them fight. Teach one decision frame: **Protect → Plan → Play** (essentials first, goals second, fun with what remains — and fun DOES get a line; a budget with no joy is a budget you'll quit).

**Budget round 1 (35–75).** The total must equal exactly $1,300. Circulate hard.
**What to listen for:** food numbers that can't survive a month; $0 entertainment (call it: *"Show me a month you spent zero on fun. This budget is fiction."* — said with a grin, it lands as honesty, not shame); whether savings survives contact with wants.

**Event rounds (85–145).** Two hits, chosen by you. Give harder cards (hours cut, family needs help) to steadier teams. After each: rebudget to $1,300 and one written WHY sentence.
**Coaching move:** when a team is stuck, don't solve — narrow: *"You can't protect all five. Rank them, top three survive."*

**Gallery walk (145–170).** Budgets on the wall. One sticky per team on every other team's budget: something smart OR a risk they're missing. Enforce student-safe language: "hard to picture how this covers food" — yes; "trash budget" — no.

**Platform checkpoint (170–200).** `/studio/lab/financial-literacy/budget-builder`: students enter THEIR OWN split (not the team's — this is the individual capture), and **save My Budget Plan**. The module's needs/wants/savings/giving percentages formalize what they just fought about for two hours.

**Coach clinic (200–220).** Picks based on the room: the 50/30/20 idea (as a starting point, not a law), why fixed costs feel invisible, subscriptions as leaks, cash-envelope tricks that still work.

**Defenses (220–240).** 60-second defenses: protected / cut / what $200 more changes. Score. Exit ticket: *"What did you protect, and what does that say about what you value?"*

### Common misconceptions
- "Budgeting means no fun." — A budget without a fun line fails in week two. Plan the fun.
- "I'll remember where it went." — Nobody does. That's the whole point of writing it down.
- "Helping at home means I can't get ahead." — Reframe: it's a line item like any other; plans that include it are still plans, and it's temporary leverage both directions.

### If time runs short
Cut the gallery walk to a stand-and-scan (5 min, no stickies). Never cut the second event card — one shock teaches adjustment, two teach systems.

### If tech fails
Budget Builder becomes homework/tomorrow's opener; the paper budget grid in the packet IS the artifact today.

### Collect
Team budget charts (photo), exit tickets, the WHY sentences — read those; they tell you who's actually thinking in tradeoffs.

# DAY 3 — Credit Can Help You or Trap You

**Big idea:** credit is a tool with a price tag; structure decides whether it builds you or bleeds you. "Looks affordable" and "is affordable" are different numbers.

**Learning objectives.** Students can: (1) compute the true cost of a BNPL/minimum-payment/high-APR structure; (2) explain utilization, payment history, and debt-to-income in plain language; (3) sort credit offers into smart/risky/trap using numbers.

**Materials:** packets, the four mini-cases, a board or projector for the minimum-payment demo, debate space.

### Time breakdown (240 min)

| Min | Block |
|---|---|
| 0–15 | Hook: the sneaker offer |
| 15–40 | Mini-lesson: how credit actually works |
| 40–80 | Credit Choice Tournament (cases A–D) |
| 80–90 | **Break** |
| 90–110 | Minimum Payment Trap demo |
| 110–140 | Debate: "Is credit bad?" |
| 140–160 | Scenario sorting + Rewrite the Rule |
| 160–190 | **Platform checkpoint: Credit & Debt** |
| 190–210 | Coach clinic |
| 210–240 | Credit Rulebooks share-out + scoreboard + exit ticket |

### Facilitator script & moves

**Hook (0–15).** Project or read the exact BNPL pitch: *"4 interest-free payments of $45. No credit check. Get them today."* Ask: *"What's the catch? There's always a catch — find it."* (Catches: late fees, autopay overdrafts, the habit itself, and $180 you didn't have is still $180 you don't have.)

**Mini-lesson (15–40).** Plain-language pass: interest (rent on money), minimum payment (the option designed to keep you paying), payment history (the biggest score factor — boring on-time beats everything), utilization (using $190 of a $200 limit looks desperate even if you pay it), debt-to-income (the landlord/lender math from tomorrow). One sentence each on the board.

**Tournament (40–80).** Teams work cases A–D in the packet: verdict SMART/RISKY/TRAP + the number that proves it. Bracket-style if the room likes competition: teams defend verdicts against each other, you referee with math.
**What to listen for:** Case B (secured card) getting dismissed as boring — that's the teachable moment: *"Boring is what building credit looks like. Exciting is what traps look like."* Case C: make them compute total paid (~$7,100) vs. sticker ($4,500). Case D: there is no clean answer — score the REASONING.

**Minimum Payment Trap demo (90–110).** $600 at 27% with $20 minimums. Have teams guess months and total first, then walk it: roughly **4 years and ~$960 total** — for a $600 balance, if nothing else is ever charged. Let the room react. Then show the same debt at $60/month: gone in ~11 months, ~$80 interest. The lesson is one sentence: **the minimum is not a suggestion for you — it's a business model for them.**

**Debate (110–140).** Split the room by assignment (not belief): "credit is bad" vs. "credit is a tool." 3-minute prep, 1-minute openings, rebuttals, your cross-examination with numbers. Then drop roles: *"Real answer, together: credit isn't good or bad — structure is. What structure makes it safe?"*

**Rewrite the Rule (140–160).** Teams finish: *"Before I sign up to pay later, I will always ______."* and draft their 5-rule Credit Rulebook.

**Platform checkpoint (160–190).** `/studio/lab/financial-literacy/credit-debt`: run the module's numbers, **save the reflection** — require their #1 rule from the Rulebook to appear in it.

**Coach clinic (190–210).** Picks: what's actually in a credit score, secured cards / becoming an authorized user (with a trusted adult — flag the trust part), why closing your oldest card can hurt, payday loans in Michigan (the fee math from Case D, annualized out loud).

**Share-out (210–240).** Each team reads their safest option, their riskiest option, and one rule. Exit ticket: *"Your personal credit rule, one sentence, your words."*

### Common misconceptions
- "No credit = good credit." — Invisible is its own problem (role card 8, and tomorrow's landlord).
- "BNPL isn't debt." — It's debt with better marketing.
- "Checking my score hurts it." — Checking your own is free and harmless; hard pulls are the ones that count, lightly.
- "One missed payment is no big deal." — Payment history is the heaviest factor; one 30-day late can follow you for years.

### If time runs short
Compress the debate to one round of openings + your synthesis. Never cut the minimum-payment demo — it's the single highest-impact 20 minutes of the week.

### If tech fails
The demo works on a whiteboard. Credit & Debt module rolls into Day 4's checkpoint block (they'll have two modules there anyway).

### Collect
Credit Rulebooks (these are gold — quote them Thursday), exit tickets.

# DAY 4 — Could You Really Move Out?

**Big idea:** independence has a sticker price and a hidden price; the strongest move might be the patient one — and that's a strategy, not a failure.

**Learning objectives.** Students can: (1) compute total move-in cash including deposit, utilities, furniture, and emergency fund; (2) explain what landlords screen for (3× income, credit, history) and the workarounds (co-signer, bigger deposit, pay-history letter); (3) build and pitch a 12-month money plan for their chosen path.

**Materials:** packets, event cards 11–16 in play, a table for the "leasing office," landlord props if you want fun (clipboard, name tag), pitch timer.

### Time breakdown (240 min)

| Min | Block |
|---|---|
| 0–15 | Hook: the listing |
| 15–35 | Mini-lesson: what the landlord sees |
| 35–70 | Case: four paths, move-in math |
| 70–80 | **Break** |
| 80–115 | Landlord qualification role play |
| 115–125 | Event card hits (11–14) |
| 125–150 | 12-Month Money Plan build |
| 150–190 | **Platform checkpoint: Renting & Ownership + My Money Plan** |
| 190–215 | Money Plan pitches (2 min/team) + peer feedback |
| 215–230 | Coach clinic / week synthesis |
| 230–240 | Final scoreboard + exit ticket |

### Facilitator script & moves

**Hook (0–15).** Read a real-sounding listing: *"1BR, $950/mo. First month + security deposit due at signing. Income must be 3× rent. Credit check required."* Ask: *"You make $16/hour full-time. Can you even apply? Don't guess — someone do the math out loud."* ($16 × 40 × 52 / 12 = $2,773 gross monthly; 3 × $950 = $2,850. **They miss the screen by $77.** Let that hang.)

**Mini-lesson (15–35).** The landlord's checklist: income 3× rent (usually GROSS — one break for the applicant), credit history, prior evictions, deposit cash. Then the workarounds, taught as tools: co-signer (what it really asks of the co-signer), bigger deposit, pay-history letter (phone bill, at-home payments — proof you pay on time when the file is thin). Then move-in math preview: first month + deposit + utility setup + furniture minimum + first month's food + transportation + an emergency fund you don't touch.

**Case build (35–70).** Each team is assigned one path (Stay/Roommates/Solo/Alternative) and builds it honestly — including the paths they didn't choose. Move-in math table must be completed with real estimates.
**What to listen for:** teams treating Path 1/4 as "losing" — intervene early: *"The person who stays home 12 months with $4,000 saved is not behind the person eating ramen in a $950 apartment. Run both numbers 12 months out."*

**Role play (80–115).** You (or a second adult) are the landlord at the leasing office table. Each team sends applicants with their path's numbers. Ask real questions: income? proof? deposit? who's the co-signer and do they know? Approve, conditionally approve (bigger deposit / co-signer / come back in 6 months), or decline — always with the sentence: *"Here's what makes this a yes in 6 months."* Keep it warm, keep it real. The room learns from every interview.

**Event cards (115–125).** Cards 11–14 (rent increase, roommate short, utility deposit, credit check bounce) hit the relevant teams. Roommate-short is the best conversation of the week — let it breathe: *"Your name is on the lease too. What are your options that keep both the apartment AND the friendship?"*

**12-Month Plan (125–150).** Whatever the path: monthly savings target, credit moves (secured card from Day 3?), income moves, and the calendar date they could hand a landlord a strong application — or hit the alternative goal.

**Platform checkpoint (150–190).** Two modules, in order: `/studio/lab/financial-literacy/renting-homeownership` (save it), then `/studio/lab/financial-literacy/money-plan` — **the capstone**. My Money Plan pulls the whole week into one saved document: income estimate (Day 1), budget split (Day 2), credit takeaways (Day 3), housing goal (today), one habit, one open question. Insist the "one question I still need answered" field is real — it feeds Friday and your follow-up.

**Pitches (190–215).** 2 minutes, every teammate speaks, template in the packet. Peer feedback: one strength + one "hard to picture" per pitch.

**Coach clinic / synthesis (215–230).** Tie the week: check → budget → credit → keys. The through-line: *"Every day this week was the same skill — deciding on purpose before life decides for you."*

**Close (230–240).** Final scoreboard, winner announced (loudly), exit ticket: *"The honest date you could move out — and are you okay with it?"*

### Common misconceptions
- "3× rent is on take-home." — Usually gross; know which number a screen uses.
- "The deposit is the only upfront cost." — Move-in math routinely doubles it.
- "A co-signer is a formality." — It's someone betting their credit on you; treat it that way.
- "Staying home means failing to launch." — It can be the fastest route to launching well.

### If time runs short
Cut clinic to 5 minutes and trim pitch feedback. Never cut My Money Plan — the week must end saved.

### If tech fails
Pitches proceed on paper; both modules become a scheduled make-up block (they are the week's artifact — do not let them silently drop).

### Collect
Pitch sheets, final scoreboard, exit tickets, and confirm in Studio that drafts exist for your roster (spot-check, don't surveil).

# FRIDAY — Field Trip: Money in the Real World

**Before boarding:** hand out the observation guide (`/studio/curriculum/financial-literacy-field-trip-guide.md`), review the first three prompts out loud, assign a "cost detective" per team (their job: spot the hidden cost nobody else sees).

**On site:** students observe and fill prompts 1–6. Your job is one nudge per group: *"Where's the money moving right now?"*

**After (bus or back at base):** prompts 7–9 + the 5–8 sentence Field Trip Money Reflection. If devices and time allow, students add the reflection to **My Money Plan** in Studio; otherwise collect paper and have them add it Monday.

**Debrief question for the ride home:** *"What financial decision would you make differently after this week?"* — accept only specific answers.

# Week-long logistics

- **Feedback to collect for the program:** daily exit tickets, the open questions from My Money Plan (these are your next-clinic agenda), final scoreboard, and your own 5-minute end-of-day notes on what ran long/short.
- **Optional extensions (fast finishers, any day):** compute the case at their actual wage; build the "$200 more" budget; price a real apartment listing's move-in math; draft a savings auto-transfer plan.
- **The platform's role, one more time:** the app is where work gets SAVED, not where learning happens. If you ever find the room quietly clicking for an hour, the day has inverted — close the laptops and deal an event card.
