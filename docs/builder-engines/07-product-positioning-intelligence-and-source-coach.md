# Product Positioning Intelligence And Source Coach

Product Positioning Intelligence is the bridge between Market Fit Builder and Brand Fit Builder. It helps students understand what kind of product they are actually working with, what similar products are useful comparisons, which customer profiles are most plausible, and what evidence should be gathered next.

The Source Coach is the evidence layer inside that work. It tells students what information is missing, where to look for it, and how that source can support or weaken a claim. It must not invent data.

## Purpose

Product Positioning Intelligence should help students answer:

- What kind of product is this really?
- What similar products should we compare against?
- What customer profile does this product naturally point toward?
- What price range is defensible?
- What evidence would prove or weaken that assumption?
- What data should we gather next, and where should we look?

This layer should not ask students to pick a target market from a list and stop there. It should help them infer likely markets and positioning from product reality and evidence.

Core logic:

- Product specs affect quality perception.
- Quality affects price expectations.
- Price affects customer fit.
- Customer fit affects channel.
- Channel affects design and message.
- Evidence determines how confident the team should be.

## Product Scan Framework

The product scan starts with facts students can observe, source, or defend.

Inputs:

- Product description.
- Product specs.
- Material or ingredients.
- Production method.
- Made-in or local story.
- Price.
- Estimated cost if known.
- Quality level.
- Design or style notes.
- Brand identity notes.
- Intended customer.
- Intended channel.
- Production constraints.

Outputs:

- Likely product category behavior.
- Likely price expectation.
- Quality proof needed.
- Likely customer profiles.
- Channel implications.
- Risk flags.

## Product Category Behavior Examples

A product can behave differently depending on price, quality, story, and channel.

Useful behavior labels:

- Basic school merch.
- Premium school merch.
- Streetwear.
- Detroit-made apparel.
- Giftable identity product.
- Values-based purchase.
- Boutique retail product.
- Impulse campus product.
- Bakery or food impulse product.
- Donation or supporter product.

These are not permanent labels. They are working hypotheses students should test.

Example:

A $100 sweatshirt made 100% in Detroit should not be compared only to basic school merch. It may behave more like premium local apparel, values-based apparel, giftable identity apparel, limited-run streetwear, or Detroit-made goods. Students may be the easiest audience to reach, but adults, alumni, staff, Detroit supporters, gift buyers, or premium casualwear buyers may have stronger price fit.

## Comparable Product Scan Framework

Students should enter comparable products, but the engine should evaluate why each comp is or is not useful.

Comp types:

- Product or category comp.
- Price comp.
- Quality comp.
- Brand story comp.
- Target customer comp.
- Channel comp.
- Style or design comp.
- Local-made comp.

## Strong Comps

A strong comp matches at least one important decision question.

Strong comps usually share:

- Similar product type.
- Similar price range.
- Similar quality level.
- Similar production story.
- Similar customer.
- Similar channel.
- Similar design category.
- Clear source link or source name.

A comp does not need to match everything. Students should name what it proves and what it does not prove.

## Weak Comps

A weak comp can still be useful if students understand the limits.

Weak comps often:

- Match the product category but not the price.
- Match the style but not the customer.
- Match the customer but not the production story.
- Match the brand story but not the channel.
- Come from a brand students wear but not a brand targeting the same buyer.
- Lack a source, price, or product details.

Weak comps should not be used as proof. They can become prompts for better research.

## Basic School Hoodie Vs Detroit-Made Premium Sweatshirt

A basic school hoodie comp may help students understand school identity, spirit wear, and student reach. It is weak for a 100% Detroit-made premium sweatshirt if the price, material, production method, and target buyer are different.

A Detroit-made premium sweatshirt comp may be stronger for price, quality, production story, and adult buyer fit. It may be weaker for school reach if the audience is not connected to Renaissance.

The engine should explain:

- "This comp is useful for school identity, but weak for premium price."
- "This comp is useful for local production and price, but weak for student reach."
- "Find one comp for school-spirit behavior and one comp for Detroit-made premium behavior."

## Student-Worn Brands Vs Targeted Buyers

Students may wear Lululemon, Nike, premium streetwear, or similar brands. That does not automatically mean students are the strategic target customer.

The engine should teach the difference between:

- Who wears or likes a product.
- Who can afford it.
- Who the brand is targeting.
- Who the channel can reach.
- Who is most likely to convert.

Example:

Lululemon may be useful as a premium-positioning reference, but students wearing it does not prove students are the target market. Students should identify who the brand is built to attract and compare that to the intended buyer.

## Customer Profile Framework

Product Positioning Intelligence should produce student-friendly profiles that resemble consumer segment logic without requiring paid data integration.

Each profile should include:

- Profile name.
- Age range.
- Likely income range.
- Geography or urban/suburban context.
- Lifestyle or context.
- Motivation.
- Price sensitivity.
- Values.
- Likely channel.
- Evidence needed.
- Risk.
- Validation step.

Example profile names:

- School Spirit Buyer.
- Proud Parent Supporter.
- Alumni Legacy Buyer.
- Civic Premium Buyer.
- Local Craft Buyer.
- Gift Buyer.
- Premium Casualwear Buyer.
- Impulse Campus Buyer.
- Mission Supporter / Donor.

For a $100 100%-Detroit-made House Phoenix sweatshirt, likely profiles may include Civic Premium Buyer, Proud Parent Supporter, Alumni Legacy Buyer, Gift Buyer, or Premium Casualwear Buyer. Renaissance students may still be a launch, awareness, or validation market.

## Source Coach Framework

The Source Coach tells students what information is needed and where to find it. It should not invent data, fill in missing numbers, or treat examples as proof.

Source categories:

- Student survey.
- Parent or adult interview.
- Alumni or staff interview.
- Census / ACS income, age, and geography data.
- School-provided enrollment data.
- Comparable product research.
- Retail observation.
- Phoenix Nest stakeholder feedback.
- TechTown pop-up feedback.
- Preorder or expression-of-interest test.

For each source category, the coach should define:

- What question it answers.
- When to use it.
- What students should record.
- How it supports or weakens a claim.

## Source Category Guidance

Student survey:

- Answers: what students like, would wear, might buy, or would recommend.
- Use when: students may be buyers, awareness audience, or validators.
- Record: sample size, question wording, answer counts, date, and key comments.
- Supports or weakens: student interest, design preference, price tolerance, and awareness potential.

Parent or adult interview:

- Answers: whether adults would buy, gift, donate, or support the product.
- Use when: price is higher than normal student spending or the product is giftable.
- Record: interviewee type, age range if appropriate, price reaction, motivation, objections, and quote notes.
- Supports or weakens: adult willingness to pay, gift logic, supporter logic, and price fit.

Alumni or staff interview:

- Answers: whether school connection, legacy, or mission increases willingness to buy.
- Use when: the product depends on school pride, nostalgia, or institutional support.
- Record: relationship to school, reaction to story, price reaction, channel preference, and objections.
- Supports or weakens: alumni/staff fit and school-identity positioning.

Census / ACS income, age, and geography data:

- Answers: affordability context for a geography and age group.
- Use when: students need to estimate spending-power context for adult buyers or local supporters.
- Record: geography, year, income bracket, age group, source link/name, and what the number proves.
- Supports or weakens: affordability context, not demand.

School-provided enrollment data:

- Answers: size of the reachable school audience.
- Use when: students are modeling school awareness, school launch, or student buyer potential.
- Record: enrollment count, grade bands, source name, date, and any limits.
- Supports or weakens: reachable audience inside school.

Comparable product research:

- Answers: price, quality, positioning, channel, and category expectations.
- Use when: students need outside context before defending price or positioning.
- Record: brand/product, price, product specs, channel, target customer, source link/name, and why it is similar or different.
- Supports or weakens: price defensibility, category behavior, and positioning.

Retail observation:

- Answers: what similar products look like in real retail settings.
- Use when: preparing for Phoenix Nest, TechTown, or display decisions.
- Record: store or booth, product type, price, packaging, display, customer behavior, and date.
- Supports or weakens: retail readiness, packaging, display, and price expectations.

Phoenix Nest stakeholder feedback:

- Answers: whether the product and story fit retail carry.
- Use when: writing the Phoenix Nest carry pitch.
- Record: stakeholder name/role, feedback, objections, required changes, and next step.
- Supports or weakens: retail carry fit and pitch readiness.

TechTown pop-up feedback:

- Answers: what real shoppers notice, ask, buy, or reject.
- Use when: testing launch demand and customer language.
- Record: shopper comments, questions, conversion, objections, and product interest.
- Supports or weakens: pop-up demand, message clarity, and buyer profile.

Preorder or expression-of-interest test:

- Answers: whether people will take an action before full production.
- Use when: inventory risk is high or price is uncertain.
- Record: number of signups, price shown, audience, channel, date range, and follow-through.
- Supports or weakens: demand confidence and inventory planning.

Example:

Question: Can enough people afford a $100 sweatshirt?  
Source: Census / ACS income data plus adult buyer interviews.  
Record: geography, year, income bracket, age group, source link/name, and what the number proves.  
Caution: income data does not prove willingness to buy; it only supports affordability context.

## Quantification Path

Students need a path from profile to countable market.

Steps:

1. Choose likely profile.
2. Define geography.
3. Define age range.
4. Define income or spending-power proxy.
5. Find public data source.
6. Estimate reachable audience.
7. Estimate interest rate.
8. Estimate conversion rate.
9. State confidence.
10. Name validation step.

The engine should distinguish:

- Total market: everyone who could theoretically fit.
- Reachable market: people the team can actually reach through a channel.
- Interested market: people likely to care after seeing the product and story.
- Likely buyers: people expected to convert at the proposed price.

Students should not use total market as likely buyers.

## Model Critique Boundaries

The critique layer may:

- Critique assumptions.
- Explain tradeoffs.
- Suggest data sources.
- Point out weak comps.
- Help translate raw data into student-friendly customer profiles.
- Suggest validation steps.
- Help improve positioning language.

The critique layer must not:

- Invent market size.
- Claim a segment exists without evidence.
- Make approval decisions.
- Move work into review.
- Overwrite student source notes.
- Replace Census / ACS or survey research.
- Make final inventory decisions.
- Treat commercial segment examples as facts unless sourced.

## Student-Facing Examples

Example 1:

"Your product is priced like premium local apparel, not basic school merch. Students are highly reachable, but you need evidence they will pay $100. Add adult buyer interviews or parent/alumni willingness-to-pay data."

Example 2:

"This comparable product is useful for category, but weak for price and story. Find one product with similar local production or premium positioning."

Example 3:

"Lululemon may be useful as a premium-positioning reference, but students wearing it does not prove students are the target market. Identify who the brand is built to attract and compare that to your intended buyer."

Example 4:

"Income data can help estimate affordability, but it does not prove demand. Pair ACS data with surveys, interviews, or preorder tests."

## Implementation Guidance

Product Positioning Intelligence should eventually connect to:

- Market Fit Builder: profile naming, comp quality, source gaps, and positioning logic.
- Brand Fit Builder: reference-brand perception, identity signals, and price-fit questions.
- Model critique: temporary review of product specs, comps, profiles, and source gaps.
- Structured evidence: claims, sources, assumptions, risks, confidence, and validation steps.
- Ch. 7 Current Product Line and Pricing: product category, price fit, and market recommendation.
- Ch. 8 Finance and Revenue Model: defensible demand assumptions and revenue scenarios.
- Ch. 10 Marketing and Campaign Playbook: audience, channel, message, and validation.
- Ch. 11 Phoenix Nest Retail Carry Pitch: carry-readiness evidence and buyer logic.
- Ch. 12 Strategy and Next-Semester Recommendations: unresolved evidence gaps and next tests.

Do not prescribe exact code before the next implementation pass. Recommended phases:

1. Add profile naming and comp alignment guidance to Market Fit Builder.
2. Add Source Coach prompts to evidence requests.
3. Add Brand Fit Builder with reference-brand perception analysis.
4. Expand model critique so it can read product specs, comps, profiles, and source gaps.
5. Add compact Playbook preview outputs.

## V1 Guardrails

This layer should stay advisory and evidence-bound:

- No automatic final text writing.
- No automatic source creation.
- No external data import in V1.
- No hidden market-size calculations from unsourced assumptions.
- No changes to submit gates.
- No changes to Playbook readiness.
- No changes to approval, task, deliverable, or blocked status.
- No sales, checkout, refund, tax, or inventory-decrement behavior.
